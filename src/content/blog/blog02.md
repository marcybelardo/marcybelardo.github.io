The multiplicities of discourse surrounding AI have a lot to do with the fact that there are many artificial intelligences, experienced by different sectors, classes, cultures, and nations. AI as experienced by the permanently employed tech worker in San Francisco is much different from the AI as experienced by a contractual freelance article writer working remotely from the Philippines. It is along these lines that issues are fought over, the building of data centers, the layoffs, the slop art, the soul of creation. In essence, one's reaction to what AI promises is shaped by one's relationship to labor.

The more I research AI, the more I interact with communities welcoming and hostile to the technology, the more certain I become in my assessments. For instance, I don't believe people who currently write or create art will ever truly become AI users. For one thing, I still believe they are better than the AI at what they do. Moreover, in lines of work where one's experiences and point-of-view take precedent (making commissioned art, writing articles or conducting interviews, etc.) there is little argument for AI's usefulness. I can hear a proponent argue that they would be good for tackling the main writing of text, but most people who are well-practiced with a keyboard or pen will be hesitant to abandon their tools. They are also quite particular about their words, their cadence, the things that distinguish them from everyone else. AI writes, but often flatly, often without flourish or idiosyncrasy. Where the manipulation of language is minute, like someone who renders a photorealistic drawing, delegating to software misses the point entirely.

Where I see AI becoming a norm is in the mass of day-to-day work humans conduct. Corporations generate text by the ton, in memos and meetings, emails, missives, messages, social media posts, SEO articles, on and on and on. The bulk of this text isn't meant to be a final product, but rather facilitators of operations, a means to an end. Much of it is written out, delivered, acted upon, and abandoned. Generative AI works wonders in this space, this corpo machine where emails nobody writes become emails nobody reads. Even when they are of relative importance, quarterly reports or research papers are not important for the text but rather the data they try to convey. Academia is warming up to AI for this very reason. Anybody with eyes and a brain can review sources and make adjustments to hallucinated information. But the base work, the pages and pages of elucidation, can be relegated.

I hate to make declarations like "AI is inevitable", but to be realistic, I do think that there's a place for AI for most people working. As I saw someone comment on Bluesky, once they make it easily usable within Excel, the fight will mostly be over. It's an unfortunate reality, but most people don't care about the words they're paid to write. Most artists doing corporate graphic design aren't passionate about the social media posts they're paid to lay out. Of course, some measure of taste is there, some pleasure in the execution of tasks one has practiced well. But the fact of the matter is that we're not all authors or fine artists. Most of the work that AI can do today is good enough for corporate use. What this does, I think, is not necessarily devalue the work done by people in the Global South, but rather expose the precarity of labor most of the world engages in.

Personally, I haven't had a full-time job for several years now. Many of my friends are the same, working freelance or temporary roles here and there to make up a full month's salary. This is despite the fact that we all went to prestigious local universities, with a few of us holding accolades and awards related to our work. It is, I think, a reality of our modern times that work in the Philippines is a slog, where corporate work is underpaid and the commutes are so hellish that one would take flaky remote jobs over the office just to avoid it. This, however, leads to a labor situation in which many are reliant on temporary foreign employment to meet a decent standard of living. I was one of these people, working for an American startup on a freelance basis until I had to leave for Turkey in 2023. So much of the Philippine economy is supported by call centers, Overseas Filipino Workers, remote positions, global employment. What is attractive about this is that it saves costs for foreign employers to take on Filipino workers over domestic ones. But by building our economy around these industries, we have made ourselves vulnerable. When your job's existence is already a cost-cutting measure, then you aren't a valuable, stable building block for their success.
