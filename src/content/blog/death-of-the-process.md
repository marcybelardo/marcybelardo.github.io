Riemann has been proven, and mathematicians have become philosophers. Following the Lean and Mathlib community's endorsement of Levent Alpöge and Claude's proof, we are now forced to face the fact that important mathematical discoveries are squarely within the province of automation. Seventeen million lines of code now sit before us as an Everest, a nearly insurmountable climb. At the proverbial base camp, the question is being asked: *what the fuck is the point?*

What is the value of a mathematical proof? Opinions are mixed. The oft-cited hypothetical cancer cure is valuable whether or not a human creates it, but it's not like Riemann is getting pumped into anyone's veins anytime soon [^1]. Rather, mathematics is touted as a product of the *mind*—abstract and beyond mere commodification. In Reddit threads and Hacker News comment sections, the claim is being laid down that the value of a proof is not necessarily in its truth value, but on its side effects: new fields of mathematics, as well as new mathematicians. The proof is, by their account, a nice little bonus. The conjecture matters because of who it inspires, and what structures its proof necessitates. As Alpöge put it himself, "a principal guiding light".

Of course, mathematics is not the only industry facing such crisis. Numbers from the [U.S. Census Bureau](https://www.census.gov/library/working-papers/2026/adrm/CES-WP-26-56.html), [Stanford Digital Economy Lab](https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/publication/canaries-in-the-coal-mine-six-facts-about-the-recent-employment-effects-of-artificial-intelligence/), and [Federal Reserve Bank of New York](https://www.newyorkfed.org/research/college-labor-market#--:explore:unemployment) all report a decrease in computer science graduate unemployment and decreases in new junior hiring[^2]. For the sake of argument, I'm going to assume all this is true, and is because of AI. If that is so, then we are reckoning with a dramatic shift in our relationship to knowledge work. How so? In her article [_Generative AI is not going to build your engineering team for you_](https://stackoverflow.blog/2024/12/31/generative-ai-is-not-going-to-build-your-engineering-team-for-you/), Charity Majors argues that the software industry is an apprenticeship industry, one whose value is in the production of engineers rather than simply as the production of software. 

> Writing code is but a sliver of what professional software engineers do. Only we have the context and the credibility to drive the changes we _know_ form the bedrock for great teams and engineering excellence.

In his article [_The Next Two Years of Software Engineering_](https://addyosmani.com/blog/next-two-years/), Addy Osmani stresses that universities struggle to keep up with the rapid change of pace, and that junior (and senior) engineers must rewrite their strategies for how to work with AI. In any case, it seems more and more likely that the old world is gone.

A concept I've come back to over and over recently is Process art. Primarily influenced by Abstract Expressionist ideas, particularly the techniques of painters like Jackson Pollock, it is a creative movement that seeks to center the artistic process, the material interaction between artist and work, as the central concern rather than the art object itself[^3]. In this way, the making of the work becomes a part of, or even the entirety of the artistic focus. This, I feel, connects deeply with the sentiments I've heard echoed in math and tech communities post-AI. I can attest also, as I have interacted plenty with the fine arts, that the creation of practicing artists is highly valued by the art community writ large.

Kim Grant in her book _All About Process_ (2017) describes this focus on process as "the central cliché of artist statements in recent decades". She goes on to describe how many artists used this as a kind of response to artworks becoming mere commodities.

> In broad terms, the artist’s process becomes the site for a distinctly human, nonutilitarian, purposeful activity of immense value in itself. Its products are mere traces and remains of that physical and mental activity; they are in themselves of little to no value to their creator. In this way the artist is freed, at least theoretically, from directly engaging in commodity production.

Roger Sansi, writing about _The Artist and the Stone_[^4], remarks that process is difficult to fully entangle from product. It may indeed be that the process itself becomes a product as well.

This is where I get hung up, when I hear about all this call to refocus on processes and creators. The software engineering industry was probably the last place I was expecting to hear it from. Certainly, I don't want to discount the importance of practitioners or process in-and-of-themselves, but surely what is produced matters, right? Coders who write assembly by hand are valorized, but I see very little adoption of assembly by most programmers relative to the valorization. Conversely, I see artists who reject those foundations. Figure drawing and value studies are taught in the fine arts but many artists rebel, walking directly into the abstract.
